What we would do again

The ledger is the full record. Two trades are here because they are the ones we mean to use. Anterix returned $6,635.63, a gain of 94.95 percent, and we would still sell sooner. Amtech lost $548.35, or 23.25 percent, and the sale is the part we would make again. The profit and the loss are evidence. The grade is the decision.

The second buy had to be earned.

Anterix · +$6,635.63 · +94.95%

Anterix owns licenses in the 900 MHz band. We bought it in April because the FCC was opening that band to broader broadband use, and we thought the licenses could support a price about twice what we paid. That was specific enough to be wrong. We can check the target. That is the kind of idea we want on the way in.

We buy in pieces. A full position is about a fifth of the account, and the second purchase has to be earned by the price. On April 17 we bought 83 shares for $3,518. The exit is the part we would change. The market had already given us a reason to sell. We sold 40 shares in July and the remaining 121 across three sales in September. Net proceeds were $13,623.83 on $6,988.20 of cost. The gain stands. Execution is a 3 because we were slow. The trigger to sell has to be written with the same specificity as the trigger to add. Amtech Systems · −$548.35 · −23.25%

Micron, Sandisk, SK Hynix, and Samsung ran hard, and a lot of the equipment stocks moved with them. Those four supply most of the world’s memory, and the price tends to move together. We did not own them. The memory stocks ran without us. That is a fact about the book. Buying a smaller company afterward, so the book feels caught up, is how a missed move turns into a bad trade.

While a buildout is going in, the company that sells the ties can look as if demand will last as long as the track. When the track is finished, the orders for ties fall off. Druckenmiller has used that comparison for years. The same comparison is being made now about data centers, memory, and the equipment that builds them. It is worth keeping. It says to own the companies getting the orders, or to leave the theme alone.

The money was real. Capital was moving into advanced packaging, the step that stacks and connects chips. Amtech was talking about business with a hyperscaler, one of the large cloud companies, and it looked like a turnaround those orders could carry. We bought it on June 15 for $2,358.63. We were early, and we were in the wrong stock. Amtech’s growth slowed. The large packaging companies kept taking business from the smaller ones. In much of semiconductor equipment, two or three companies end up owning the market because they keep the orders. We were out by June 30. The loss was $548.35, or 23.25 percent. The sale is the decision we would repeat. The purchase is the one we would retire. The idea is a 2. The sale is a 3. The Amtech note

What we hold ourselves to

Four tests. A trade can pass one and fail another. The grades in the ledger are where that shows up.

Transparency

The partnership account is on this page in a form a reader can check. Each closed trade carries what we paid, what we received, and the reason we bought and sold. A holding we still own is named and kept out of the total. Riskified is that holding. The $20,986.17 is closed trades only. The individual account is excluded from every figure. A number on this page is a number we can show from the brokerage record.

Integrity

A gain and a loss are written in the same detail, and the grade stays on the decision. Anterix was the second-largest gain in the account. The exit is a 3, because we sold after the market had already made the case for leaving. Amtech lost 23.25 percent. The sale is a 3, because getting out followed the evidence we had. The result sits beside the grade. The decision sets the grade.

Convexity

We want the loss, if we are wrong, to be smaller than the gain if we are right, and we want to know beforehand what being wrong looks like. Nebius had a defined downside and a larger upside, which is why we owned it. Anterix was a claim that the licenses could be worth about twice the price we paid. A trade that pays about the same for being right as it costs for being wrong is a trade we pass. We start with part of the position. We add when the price confirms the idea. We stop adding when the market is weakening and each higher purchase is only a way to stay involved. Oscar is the case where we did not stop.

Winning

The standard is a decision we would make again, including the decision to leave. Definium’s data still looked good when we sold. The stock was making a top, so we sold the shares and let the price settle the argument. In July, Planet Labs looked as if it had stopped falling. It had not. Eleven days later the levels we watch were broken, and we were out. Following the price is the work. Staying because the original story still sounds right is how a small loss becomes a trade we cannot defend.
